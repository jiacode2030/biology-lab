    Data, graphs & statistics

    Two bars of different heights do not prove two groups differ. How spread out the data are, and how many measurements there are, decide that.

    The idea

    A mean summarizes a sample, but samples vary. The standard deviation (SD) says how far individual measurements typically fall from the mean. The standard error of the mean (SEM = SD/√n) says how precisely the mean itself is known, and it shrinks as you take more measurements.

    Error bars are a way to see this. If you plot the mean ± 2 SEM (about a 95% confidence interval) for two groups and the bars do not overlap, the difference is probably real. If they overlap a lot, the data do not support a difference. Always say which kind of error bar a graph uses.

    Common mistakes

    • Using n instead of n − 1 for a sample SD.
    • Confusing SD with SEM. SD describes the data; SEM describes the mean.
    • Saying overlapping error bars prove no difference. They mean the data do not show one.
    • Comparing bar heights without looking at the error bars or the sample size.
    • Forgetting to label the axes with units, or to say what the error bars represent.

    Chi-square test

    Flip a coin 100 times and you will not get exactly 50 heads. The chi-square test asks whether a miss is small enough to be luck.

    The idea

    Genetics predicts ratios, such as 3:1. Real counts never match exactly, so we need a rule for when the miss is too big. The chi-square statistic adds up how far each observed count is from the expected count, scaled by the expected count: χ² = Σ (o − e)² / e.

    Compare χ² with a critical value that depends on the degrees of freedom (df = number of categories − 1) and a significance level, usually p = 0.05. If χ² is less than the critical value, you fail to reject the null hypothesis: the data are consistent with the expected ratio and chance can explain the difference. If χ² is greater, you reject it: the difference is probably not just chance.

    • Saying "accept the null". You can only fail to reject it.
    • Thinking a small χ² proves the hypothesis. It means chance is a sufficient explanation.

    Experimental design

    Every experiment is a fair test of one idea. If two things change at once, you cannot say which one mattered.

    The idea

    The independent variable is the one thing you change on purpose. The dependent variable is what you measure in response. A good test also has a control group that does not get the treatment (so you have something to compare with) and constants: everything else held the same, such as temperature, volume, time and source of organisms.

    Two more ideas make conclusions trustworthy. Replicates (several samples per group) let you see natural variation and compute error bars. A hypothesis is a testable prediction, often written "If ... then ... because ...". A conclusion must stay inside the data: say what changed, by how much, and what that supports, without claiming more than the design can show.

    • Changing more than one variable at a time.
    • Calling the thing you measure the independent variable.
    • Having no control group, so there is nothing to compare with.
    • Using one trial and calling the result reliable.
    • Writing a conclusion that claims more than the data show (for example proving a mechanism the experiment did not test).

    Writing FRQ answers about design

    Use the words the question uses. "Identify" means name it. "Describe" means state what you would do or see. A strong design answer names the independent variable, the dependent variable you would measure and how, a control, at least two constants, and replicates. A prediction needs a reason: "If more fertilizer is added, growth will increase up to a point, because nitrogen limits protein synthesis."

    Rates, Q10 & dilutions

    Biology is full of "how fast": how fast a reaction runs, how fast a population grows, how diluted a solution is. A few formulas cover most of it.

    The idea

    A rate is a change per unit time: rate = Δquantity / Δtime. On a graph of quantity against time, the rate is the slope. To compare conditions, measure the same change over the same time.

    The temperature coefficient Q10 says how much a rate changes for a 10 °C rise: Q10 = (R2/R1)10/(T2 − T1). Many biological rates have Q10 near 2. This only holds below the temperature that denatures the enzymes. A serial dilution makes a solution weaker in equal steps: each step multiplies the dilution by the same factor.

    Water & hydrogen bonds

    Water is a small molecule that behaves like a much bigger one. Almost every property life relies on comes from one weak attraction between water molecules.

    The idea

    A water molecule is polar: oxygen pulls electrons harder than hydrogen does, so oxygen is slightly negative and the hydrogens slightly positive. The positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the negative oxygen of another. This weak attraction is a hydrogen bond. One bond is weak and breaks easily, but liquid water has enormous numbers of them forming and breaking constantly.

    That network explains the properties on the exam. Cohesion (water sticks to water) gives surface tension and lets water be pulled up plant xylem; adhesion (water sticks to other polar surfaces) helps it climb narrow tubes. A high specific heat means water absorbs lots of energy for a small temperature change, so it buffers temperature. Ice is less dense than liquid water because bonds lock molecules into an open lattice, so ice floats. Polar and charged substances dissolve in water; nonpolar ones do not.

    Macromolecules & monomers

    Cells build enormous molecules out of a few small repeating units, and they take them apart with the same chemistry in reverse.

    The idea

    There are four classes: carbohydrates (monosaccharides join into polysaccharides: starch, glycogen, cellulose), proteins (amino acids into polypeptides), nucleic acids (nucleotides into DNA and RNA) and lipids (fats are made of glycerol and fatty acids, but lipids are not true polymers). Each has a typical function: energy and structure, catalysis and structure, information, and long-term energy and membranes.

    Monomers are joined by dehydration synthesis: each bond forms as a water molecule is removed. Polymers are broken down by hydrolysis, which uses a water molecule to split each bond. The bonds are glycosidic (sugars), peptide (amino acids), phosphodiester (nucleotides) and ester (fats).

    Protein structure

    A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into one particular shape, and the shape is what lets it work.

    The idea

    Twenty amino acids share the same backbone but differ in their R group: nonpolar (hydrophobic), polar, or charged. The order of amino acids is the primary structure. Hydrogen bonds along the backbone create secondary structure (α-helices and β-pleated sheets). Interactions among R groups (hydrophobic clustering, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges) fold the chain into a tertiary shape. Some proteins are made of several polypeptides: quaternary structure.

    Because folding depends on the R groups, changing even one amino acid can change the shape and function (sickle cell hemoglobin has one substitution). Heat, pH and salt can break the weak interactions that hold the shape: that is denaturation, and it usually destroys function.

    DNA & RNA structure

    DNA stores biological information in a simple pattern: two strands, four bases, and fixed pairing rules.

    The idea

    A nucleotide has a sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), a phosphate and a nitrogenous base. In DNA the bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C).     Cell organelles & compartments

    A eukaryotic cell is a set of rooms, each with its own job. Keeping reactions in separate compartments is what lets a cell run many incompatible processes at once.

    The idea

    The nucleus holds the DNA. Ribosomes (not membrane-bound) build proteins; those on the rough ER make proteins for export or membranes, and the smooth ER makes lipids and detoxifies.     Surface area to volume

    A cell must take in nutrients through its surface but needs them for its whole volume. As a cell grows, the volume outpaces the surface, and that limits cell size.

    The idea

    For a cube of side s: surface area = 6s², volume = s³, so SA:V = 6/s. For a sphere of radius r: SA = 4πr², V = (4/3)πr³, so SA:V = 3/r. In both cases SA:V decreases as the cell gets bigger. Doubling the side multiplies SA by 4 but volume by 8.

    This explains why cells are small, why gas exchange organs (lungs, gills, root hairs, villi) are folded or branched to add surface area, and why large organisms need transport systems. A cell of any shape exchanges materials faster when its SA:V is higher, so a flattened or elongated cell does better than a sphere of the same volume.

    Membrane structure & permeability

    A cell membrane is a thin oily film with proteins floating in it. It is selective because of what it is made of, not because it has holes.

    The idea

    The membrane is a phospholipid bilayer: each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic tails, so in water the tails hide in the middle. Proteins are embedded (integral) or attached (peripheral); carbohydrates on the outer surface help cells recognize each other.     Diffusion & osmosis

    Nothing pushes a drop of dye through water. It spreads because molecules are always moving, and random motion has a direction once there are more molecules on one side.

    The idea

    Every molecule in a liquid is jostled constantly by thermal motion. Put more dye particles on the left than on the right and, just by chance, more of them wander left-to-right than right-to-left. The net movement is from high concentration to low concentration, and it stops (with the particles still moving) when the concentrations are equal. That is diffusion, and it is passive: it takes no energy from the cell.

    Osmosis is diffusion of water across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. Water ends up moving toward the side with more solute, because that side has less free water. Compare two solutions by their solute concentration: the one with more solute is hypertonic, the one with less is hypotonic, equal is isotonic. An animal cell in a hypotonic solution swells (and can burst); in a hypertonic solution it shrinks. A plant cell has a wall: it becomes firm (turgid) in hypotonic solution and, in hypertonic solution, its membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis).

    Water potential

    Water moves toward lower water potential. The number tells you the direction and, when you compare two places, which way the cell will swell or shrink.

    The idea

    Water potential (Ψ) measures the tendency of water to move. Water always moves from higher to lower Ψ. It has two parts: solute potential Ψs = −iCRT (always zero or negative; more solute means lower) and pressure potential Ψp (positive in a turgid plant cell, zero in an open beaker). Ψ = Ψp + Ψs. Pure water in an open container has Ψ = 0.

    Active transport & bulk transport

    Moving a substance down its gradient is free. Moving it up the gradient costs energy, and cells spend a lot of ATP doing it.

    The idea

    Facilitated diffusion uses channel or carrier proteins but still moves substances down their concentration gradient: no energy needed. The sodium-potassium pump moves 3 Na⁺ out and 2 K⁺ in per ATP, keeping Na⁺ low and K⁺ high inside the cell.     Enzymes & activation energy

    Reactions in cells would be far too slow at body temperature without help. Enzymes make them fast enough to sustain life, and do it one reaction at a time.

    The idea

    An enzyme is a protein catalyst. It binds its substrate at the active site, whose shape and chemistry fit that substrate (the active site adjusts slightly on binding, induced fit). Enzymes speed reactions by lowering the activation energy (the energy hurdle to get started). They do not change the free energy of the reactants or products, do not make an unfavorable reaction favorable, and are not used up.

    Reaction rate rises with substrate concentration until all active sites are busy (saturation), then levels off at Vmax. A competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and blocks the active site; more substrate can outcompete it, so Vmax can still be reached. A noncompetitive (allosteric) inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes the shape of the enzyme, lowering the Vmax that can be reached.

    Temperature, pH & enzyme activity

    Enzymes work best in a narrow range. Move out of it and the protein changes shape and the reaction slows or stops.

    The idea

    Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and optimum pH fitted to the place it works: pepsin works at about pH 2 in the stomach, trypsin at about pH 8 in the small intestine, and the polymerase from hot-spring bacteria works near 72 °C. Rates rise with temperature because molecules collide more often and with more energy; past the optimum the enzyme denatures and the rate falls steeply.

    pH changes the charges on amino acid R groups, which changes ionic and hydrogen bonds and so the active site. Extreme pH can denature the enzyme. These effects show up on exam graphs as bell-shaped curves; the peak marks the optimum. A graph with temperature on the x-axis that rises, peaks and then drops quickly is the typical pattern.

    Cells run on one common energy currency. Many reactions that would not go on their own are driven by splitting ATP.

    The idea

    ATP (adenosine triphosphate) has an adenine base, a ribose sugar and three phosphate groups.     Photosynthesis

    Plants make sugar from air and water, and the energy for it comes from light. The process has two linked stages: one that captures light energy and one that spends it to build sugar.

    The idea

    In the light reactions (thylakoid membranes), light excites electrons in photosystems. Water is split to replace them, releasing O₂. The electrons pass along an electron transport chain, pumping H⁺ into the thylakoid space. The H⁺ gradient drives ATP synthase (chemiosmosis) to make ATP, and the electrons end up in NADPH.

    Cellular respiration

    Cells get usable energy by taking sugar apart a little at a time and capturing the energy in ATP.

    The idea

    Overall: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6 O₂ → 6 CO₂ + 6 H₂O + energy (ATP and heat). It happens in four steps. Glycolysis (cytoplasm) splits glucose into 2 pyruvate with a net gain of 2 ATP and 2 NADH. Pyruvate oxidation (mitochondrial matrix) converts each pyruvate to acetyl-CoA, releasing CO₂ and making NADH. The citric acid cycle (matrix) completes the breakdown, releasing CO₂ and making ATP, NADH and FADH₂. The electron transport chain (inner membrane) takes electrons from NADH and FADH₂, pumps H⁺ across the membrane and uses O₂ as the final electron acceptor (forming water).

    The resulting H⁺ gradient drives ATP synthase (chemiosmosis), which makes most of the ATP: roughly 30–32 per glucose in all. Without oxygen the chain stops, NADH cannot be recycled, and the cell must use fermentation instead.

    Fermentation & anaerobic respiration

    Without oxygen the electron transport chain stops, but glycolysis can keep running if the cell can recycle its NADH.

    The idea

    Glycolysis needs NAD⁺ to keep going. When oxygen is absent, the chain cannot re-oxidise NADH to NAD⁺. Some prokaryotes carry out anaerobic respiration with an acceptor other than O₂ at the end of an electron transport chain.

    Cell signaling

    Cells talk to each other with chemical messages. The message never has to enter the cell: the receiving cell converts it into an internal signal.

    The idea

    Cell communication has three stages. In reception, a signaling molecule (ligand) binds a specific receptor protein, usually on the cell surface (small nonpolar signals such as steroid hormones can cross the membrane and bind receptors inside). In transduction, the receptor changes shape and starts a chain of molecules, often a phosphorylation cascade in which protein kinases activate one another. Second messengers such as cAMP and Ca²⁺ spread the signal quickly. In the response, the cell changes what it does: turns genes on or off, activates an enzyme, or reshapes its cytoskeleton.

    Cascades amplify the signal because each activated enzyme activates many molecules in the next step, so a few ligand molecules can trigger a huge response. Signals are specific because only cells with the right receptor respond. Signals act over different distances: direct contact (cell junctions), paracrine (local), synaptic (neurons) and endocrine (hormones in blood). A mutation in any step can block or constantly activate a pathway, which is a common cause of cancer.

    Feedback & homeostasis

    Living things keep conditions steady by measuring them and correcting any drift. Occasionally they do the opposite on purpose.

    The idea

    Negative feedback reverses a change and brings a variable back to its set point: body temperature, blood glucose, blood pH and water balance. A sensor detects a change, a control center compares it with the set point, and an effector acts to oppose it. For blood glucose, high glucose makes the pancreas release insulin (cells take up glucose, liver stores glycogen); low glucose makes it release glucagon (liver releases glucose).

Examples: oxytocin and uterine contractions during childbirth, ripening fruit releasing ethylene that speeds ripening, and blood clotting. Positive feedback is not a failure of homeostasis; it drives a process to completion. Without any feedback a variable drifts toward the surroundings.

Mitosis is used for growth, repair and (in many organisms) asexual reproduction. In animal cells cytokinesis pinches the cell (cleavage furrow); in plant cells a cell plate forms.

    Cell cycle control & cancer

    Cells do not divide just because they can. A series of checkpoints decides whether a cell should go ahead, and cancer is what happens when the checkpoints fail.

    The idea

    The cycle is controlled by cyclins and cyclin-dependent kinases (CDKs). A CDK is only active when bound to its cyclin, whose level rises and falls through the cycle. Three main checkpoints stop the cycle unless conditions are right: G₁ checkpoint (is the cell big enough, are nutrients and growth factors present, is the DNA undamaged?), G₂ checkpoint (has the DNA been copied accurately?) and the M (spindle) checkpoint (are all chromosomes attached to the spindle before anaphase?). Cells that stop can enter G₀ (a resting state).

    The protein p53 halts the cycle when DNA is damaged and triggers repair, or apoptosis (programmed cell death) if the damage is too great. Cancer arises when mutations break this control. Mutations that make proto-oncogenes overactive (oncogenes) act like a stuck accelerator; mutations that disable tumor suppressor genes (such as p53) remove the brakes. Usually several mutations are needed. Cancer cells divide without signals and ignore apoptosis.

    Meiosis & genetic variation

    Sexual reproduction mixes genes from two parents. Meiosis is the cell division that halves the chromosome number and shuffles the genes so that no two gametes are alike.

    The idea

    A diploid cell (2n) has two copies of each chromosome, one from each parent (homologous chromosomes). Meiosis makes haploid (n) gametes with one copy of each. After DNA replication, meiosis I separates the homologs (so the cells become haploid) and meiosis II separates the sister chromatids (like mitosis). One diploid cell yields four genetically different haploid cells.

    Variation comes from three sources. Crossing over in prophase I swaps pieces between homologs, making new combinations of alleles on one chromosome. Independent assortment in metaphase I: each homologous pair lines up at random, so with n pairs there are 2n possible combinations (over 8 million in humans). Random fertilization then combines any egg with any sperm. Errors in separation (nondisjunction) produce gametes with extra or missing chromosomes, as in trisomy 21.

    Mendelian inheritance

    Offspring get one allele for each gene from each parent. With a Punnett square you can predict the odds before anything is born.

    The idea

    Mendel's law of segregation: the two alleles for a gene separate into different gametes, so each gamete carries one. A dominant allele shows when present; a recessive allele shows only in two copies. Genotype is the allele combination (AA, Aa, aa); phenotype is the observable trait. A cross of two heterozygotes (Aa × Aa) gives genotypes 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa and phenotypes 3 dominant : 1 recessive.

    The law of independent assortment: genes on different chromosomes are inherited independently, so AaBb × AaBb gives a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotype ratio. A test cross (unknown × homozygous recessive) reveals an unknown genotype. Use the product rule (and) and the sum rule (or) for probabilities. Real counts only approximate the ratios, which is where the chi-square test comes in.

    Beyond simple dominance

    Not every trait is a simple dominant-recessive pair. Alleles can blend, share, or be hidden by other genes.

    The idea

    Incomplete dominance: the heterozygote is intermediate (red × white snapdragons give pink; a 1 : 2 : 1 phenotype ratio in the next generation). Codominance: both alleles are fully expressed (AB blood type; a roan coat with both red and white hairs). Multiple alleles: a population can have more than two alleles of a gene (ABO blood: IA, IB and i; A and B are codominant, i is recessive).

    Polygenic traits depend on many genes and show a continuous range (height, skin colour) that forms a bell-shaped curve. Epistasis: one gene masks the expression of another (in labrador coat colour the gene for pigment deposition can hide the gene for pigment colour, giving 9 : 3 : 4). The environment also affects phenotype (hydrangea colour depends on soil pH). These patterns still follow Mendel's rules for how alleles are inherited; they differ in how alleles combine to make the phenotype.

    Genes on the X chromosome behave differently in males and females, because males have only one X.

    Linked genes & recombination

    Genes on the same chromosome tend to travel together. How often they separate tells you how far apart they are.

    The idea

    Independent assortment only applies to genes on different chromosomes. Genes close together on the same chromosome are linked: they are inherited together unless crossing over between them breaks the pairing. The offspring with the original (parental) combinations are parental types; those with new combinations are recombinants.

    The recombination frequency = recombinants / total offspring × 100%. The farther apart two genes, the more often a crossover falls between them. By definition 1% recombination = 1 map unit (centimorgan). Recombination frequencies cannot exceed 50%; at 50% the genes behave as if unlinked. Distances from three genes let you order them on a chromosome. A test cross (AaBb × aabb) makes recombinants easy to count because the recessive parent contributes only recessive alleles.

    DNA replication

    Before a cell divides it copies every base of its DNA. The copy is made by a machine that reads each old strand and builds a new partner.

    The idea

    Replication is semiconservative: each new DNA molecule has one old strand and one new strand. It starts at an origin of replication. Helicase unwinds the double helix, making a replication fork; single-strand binding proteins keep the strands apart; topoisomerase relieves twisting ahead of the fork. Primase makes a short RNA primer because DNA polymerase can only add nucleotides to an existing 3′ end. DNA polymerase then adds nucleotides only in the 5′ → 3′ direction, reading the template 3′ → 5′.

    Because the strands are antiparallel, one new strand (the leading strand) is made continuously toward the fork. The other (the lagging strand) is made away from the fork in short pieces called Okazaki fragments, each needing its own primer. DNA ligase joins the fragments after the primers are replaced with DNA. DNA polymerase also proofreads, so errors are rare (about 1 in 10⁹–10¹⁰ bases after repair).

    Transcription & RNA processing

    DNA stays in the nucleus; a working copy of one gene travels out. That copy is made of RNA.

    The idea

    Transcription makes an RNA copy of a gene. RNA polymerase binds to the promoter, unwinds a short stretch of DNA and builds an RNA strand 5′ → 3′ by pairing RNA nucleotides with the template strand (A–U, T–A, G–C, C–G). No primer is needed. Alternative splicing lets one gene make several proteins. Other RNAs (tRNA, rRNA) are also made by transcription and are not translated.

    Regulation of gene expression

    Every cell has the same genes but uses different ones. Cells switch genes on and off depending on conditions and identity.

    The idea

    In bacteria, genes with related functions are grouped in an operon with one promoter and an operator. In the lac operon (inducible), a repressor normally blocks the operator so the genes for lactose digestion are off.     Mutations

    A change in the DNA sequence may change nothing, make a protein slightly different, or ruin it completely. What happens depends on the kind of change and where it occurs.

    The idea

    A point mutation changes one base. A silent mutation changes the codon but not the amino acid (the code is redundant). A missense mutation changes one amino acid (sickle cell: GAG → GUG, glutamic acid → valine). A nonsense mutation turns an amino acid codon into a stop codon, so the protein is cut short. An insertion or deletion of bases that is not a multiple of three causes a frameshift: every codon after it is read differently, usually ruining the protein. Insertions or deletions of multiples of three add or remove amino acids without shifting the frame.

    Mutations arise from copying errors, mutagens (UV, radiation, chemicals) and mistakes in repair. They create new alleles, the raw material for evolution. Only mutations in germ cells (gametes) are passed to offspring; somatic mutations can cause cancer. Many mutations are neutral, some harmful, and a few beneficial depending on the environment.

    Biotechnology & genetic engineering

    Biologists can copy, cut, sort, read and rewrite DNA. Each technique rests on a simple property of DNA.

    Natural selection

    Populations change because individuals that happen to fit their environment better leave more offspring. Nothing in the process looks ahead.

    The idea

    Natural selection needs four things: variation in a trait, the trait is heritable, more offspring are produced than can survive, and individuals with some trait values survive and reproduce more (higher fitness). Over generations the alleles associated with those traits become more common. Individuals do not evolve; populations do. Selection acts on phenotypes, but what changes is allele frequency.

    The effect on a trait depends on which phenotypes are favored. Directional selection favors one extreme and shifts the mean (antibiotic resistance, finch beak depth in drought). Stabilizing selection favors the middle and narrows the spread (human birth weight). Disruptive selection favors both extremes and can split a population in two (seedcracker finch beaks). Other forces: sexual selection (traits that help win mates) and artificial selection (humans choose who breeds).

    Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium

    If nothing is changing allele frequencies, genotype frequencies follow a simple pattern. Departures from that pattern tell you evolution is happening.

    The idea

    For a gene with two alleles, let p be the frequency of the dominant allele and q the frequency of the recessive allele: p + q = 1. If the population is in Hardy–Weinberg equilibrium, the genotype frequencies after random mating are p² + 2pq + q² = 1 (p² homozygous dominant, 2pq heterozygous, q² homozygous recessive). Allele frequencies do not change from generation to generation.

    Equilibrium needs five conditions: very large population (no drift), random mating, no mutation, no gene flow, and no natural selection. Real populations violate them, so the equation is a null model: if observed genotype frequencies differ from expected (tested with chi-square), at least one condition is violated. Typical use: given the frequency of the recessive phenotype (q²), find q = √(q²), then p = 1 − q and the carrier frequency 2pq.

    Genetic drift & gene flow

    In small populations, chance alone can change allele frequencies, and it can even remove an allele altogether.

    The idea

    Genetic drift is random change in allele frequencies from one generation to the next, because a small sample of alleles is passed on. The smaller the population, the larger the effect; in a small population an allele can be lost or fixed (reach 100%) by chance within a few generations. Drift reduces genetic variation and does not favor adaptive alleles.

    Two common cases: the bottleneck effect (a disaster leaves only a few survivors, and the survivors' alleles are not representative; for example northern elephant seals) and the founder effect (a few individuals start a new population with a non-random sample of alleles). Gene flow (migration of alleles between populations) makes populations more similar. Mutation adds new variation; selection, drift, gene flow and mutation together drive evolution.

    Speciation & reproductive isolation

    A species is a group whose members can interbreed and produce fertile offspring. Species form when groups stop exchanging genes.

    The idea

    Under the biological species concept, species are populations that can interbreed in nature and produce viable, fertile offspring and are reproductively isolated from other groups. Isolation can be prezygotic (before a zygote forms): habitat, temporal (breeding times), behavioral (courtship), mechanical (structures do not fit), gametic (sperm and egg incompatible). Or postzygotic (after): reduced hybrid viability, reduced hybrid fertility (mule), hybrid breakdown (later generations weak).

In sympatric speciation new species form in the same area, for example by polyploidy in plants or by strong disruptive selection with mating preferences. Adaptive radiation is rapid diversification from an ancestor into many species filling different niches (Darwin's finches). Where two groups meet, hybrid zones show how complete isolation is.

    Phylogeny & cladograms

    A tree of life shows how groups are related, based on shared ancestors and the traits they inherited from them.

    The idea

    A phylogenetic tree or cladogram shows evolutionary relationships. Each node is a common ancestor; the tips are groups of organisms. A clade (monophyletic group) includes an ancestor and all of its descendants. Sister taxa share the most recent common ancestor. Groups that exclude some descendants (like "reptiles" without birds) are not clades. Position along the tree is about branching order, not about which species is "more advanced".

    Trees are built from shared derived characters (traits that appeared in a common ancestor and are inherited by its descendants) and from molecular data: the more differences in DNA or protein sequences, the longer ago two lineages diverged (a molecular clock). Homologous structures share ancestry (a bat wing and human arm); analogous structures evolved independently for similar functions (bird and insect wings) and do not show relatedness. Parsimony prefers the tree needing the fewest evolutionary changes.

    Origin of life & evidence from the past

    Life on Earth began almost 4 billion years ago, from chemistry that did not need life to start.

    The idea

    The early Earth (about 4.6 billion years ago) had little or no free oxygen and was bombarded with energy. In the Miller–Urey experiment (1953), a mixture of water vapor, methane, ammonia and hydrogen exposed to sparks produced amino acids, showing that organic molecules can form abiotically. Hypotheses: the RNA world (RNA can store information and act as a catalyst, so it may have come before DNA and proteins) and protocells (lipid vesicles with simple metabolism that could grow and divide). Eukaryotes appear about 2 billion years ago (endosymbiosis), multicellular organisms later, and most animal groups in the Cambrian about 540 million years ago. We know the timeline from fossils and radiometric dating: radioactive isotopes decay at a constant half-life, so the fraction remaining gives the age. Age = (number of half-lives) × (half-life).

    AP Unit 8

    Population growth

    A population grows quickly when resources are plentiful and levels off when they run short. The shape of the curve says what is limiting it.

    The idea

    If resources are unlimited, a population grows exponentially: the growth rate is proportional to the number of individuals, dN/dt = rmax N, where r is the per capita growth rate (births minus deaths per individual). The curve is J-shaped and gets steeper as N rises. Real populations eventually face limits, so growth follows the logistic model: dN/dt = rmax N (K − N)/K, with carrying capacity K the largest population the environment can sustain. Growth is fastest at N = K/2 and falls to zero at K.

    Density-dependent factors (competition, predation, disease, waste) slow growth more as the population gets crowded. Density-independent factors (fire, storms, freezes) affect a population regardless of its size. Species with high r and many small offspring that get little care are r-selected; species with low r, few offspring and heavy parental care are K-selected. Survivorship curves show how many individuals survive with age: Type I (most live long, die old: humans), Type II (constant death rate: many birds), Type III (high early death: oysters, most plants).

    AP Unit 8

    Communities & species interactions

    Species do not live alone. They compete, eat each other, and help each other, and those interactions shape which species live where.

    The idea

    Interactions are described by their effect on each species. Competition (−/−): two species use the same limiting resource; competitive exclusion says that if their niches are identical, one will eliminate the other, so similar species often partition resources. Predation and herbivory (+/−), parasitism (+/−), mutualism (+/+) and commensalism (+/0). A keystone species (for example the sea star Pisaster) has an effect on community structure much larger than its abundance suggests. Predators and prey often show coupled population cycles.

    Species richness is the number of species; species diversity also takes relative abundance into account. Simpson's diversity index: D = 1 − Σ(n/N)² (n = number of individuals of one species, N = total). Values near 1 mean high diversity. After a disturbance, ecological succession follows: primary (starts without soil: bare rock after a volcano or glacier) and secondary (soil remains: after a fire or abandoned field). Invasive species can outcompete natives because they lack their natural predators.

    AP Unit 8

    Energy flow & trophic levels

    Energy enters ecosystems as sunlight and leaves as heat. Only a small part of it passes from one feeding level to the next.

    The idea

    Producers (autotrophs) capture light and make organic matter; consumers (heterotrophs) eat it; decomposers break down dead material and return nutrients. Each feeding step is a trophic level. At each step most energy is lost, mainly as heat from cell respiration and in material that is not eaten or digested; typically only about 10% of the energy in one level is stored in the next. So energy pyramids narrow quickly and food chains rarely have more than four or five levels.

    Gross primary productivity (GPP) is the total energy captured by producers; net primary productivity (NPP) = GPP − R (R is the producers' respiration) is what is available to consumers. Unlike energy, nutrients cycle. A food web shows many interconnected food chains. Biomass and numbers can also be shown in pyramids, though these can be inverted (for example a pyramid of numbers with one tree and many insects).

    AP Unit 8

    Biogeochemical cycles

    Atoms are reused over and over. Carbon, nitrogen, phosphorus and water move between living things, air, water and rock.

    The idea

    Carbon cycle: producers fix CO₂ into organic molecules by photosynthesis; all organisms release CO₂ by respiration; decomposers return carbon from dead matter; burning fossil fuels and wood adds CO₂ to the atmosphere; the ocean exchanges CO₂ with the air. Nitrogen cycle: bacteria perform nitrogen fixation (N₂ → NH₃/NH₄⁺), nitrification (NH₄⁺ → NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻, the form plants take up), assimilation into proteins and nucleic acids, ammonification by decomposers and denitrification (NO₃⁻ → N₂ back to the air). Phosphorus has no gas phase: it cycles through rock, soil, water and organisms. Water cycle: evaporation, transpiration, condensation, precipitation, runoff.

    Human activities change the cycles. Burning fossil fuels raises atmospheric CO₂ (about 420 ppm in recent years, up from about 280 ppm before the industrial era), which enhances the greenhouse effect and warms the climate; some CO₂ dissolves in the oceans and causes ocean acidification. Fertilizer runoff adds nitrogen and phosphorus to waters (see eutrophication).

    AP Unit 8

    Human impact & ecosystem disruption

    Ecosystems can absorb some disturbance, but human activity often pushes them past what they can recover from.

    The idea

    Eutrophication: fertilizer or sewage adds nitrogen and phosphorus to a lake; algae bloom; when they die, decomposers use up the oxygen, and fish and other animals suffocate. Biomagnification: toxins that are fat-soluble or not broken down (DDT, mercury, PCBs) become more concentrated at each trophic level, so top predators are most affected. Invasive species can outcompete natives. Habitat fragmentation splits populations and reduces gene flow. Climate change shifts ranges and timing of life cycles, and ocean acidification harms organisms with calcium carbonate shells.

    Biodiversity matters for ecosystem stability and services. Ecosystems with more species and more genetic diversity tend to be more resilient. Conservation tools include protected areas and corridors, restoring habitat, reducing pollution, controlling invasives and sustainable harvesting. Keystone species and indicator species help monitor ecosystem health.
