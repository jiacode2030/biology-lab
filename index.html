    Math

    Data, graphs & statistics

    Two bars of different heights do not prove two groups differ. How spread out the data are, and how many measurements there are, decide that.

    The idea

    A mean summarizes a sample, but samples vary. The standard deviation (SD) says how far individual measurements typically fall from the mean. The standard error of the mean (SEM = SD/√n) says how precisely the mean itself is known, and it shrinks as you take more measurements.

    Error bars are a way to see this. If you plot the mean ± 2 SEM (about a 95% confidence interval) for two groups and the bars do not overlap, the difference is probably real. If they overlap a lot, the data do not support a difference. Always say which kind of error bar a graph uses.

      SEM falls to 1.2, so the bars separate again. More measurements give a more precise mean.

    Worked example

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Mean of A. (12+14+15+11+13)/5 = 13.
    2. Deviations squared. (−1)², 1², 2², (−2)², 0² = 1, 1, 4, 4, 0, sum 10.
    3. SD. √(10/(5−1)) = √2.5 ≈ 1.58. SEM = 1.58/√5 ≈ 0.71.
    4. Compare. B has mean 18 and the same SD, so 2 × SEM is about 1.4 for each group. 13 + 1.4 is far below 18 − 1.4, so the bars do not overlap: the treatments likely differ.

    Common mistakes

    • Using n instead of n − 1 for a sample SD.
    • Confusing SD with SEM. SD describes the data; SEM describes the mean.
    • Saying overlapping error bars prove no difference. They mean the data do not show one.
    • Comparing bar heights without looking at the error bars or the sample size.
    • Forgetting to label the axes with units, or to say what the error bars represent.

    What a graph must show

    Independent variable on the x-axis, dependent on the y-axis, both labeled with units, with an even scale. Use a line graph for continuous x (temperature, time), a bar graph for categories. For each point or bar, plot a mean and error bars when there are replicates. In an FRQ, describe the trend with numbers ("rate doubled from 20 to 30 °C"), then explain the biology.

    Chi-square test

    Flip a coin 100 times and you will not get exactly 50 heads. The chi-square test asks whether a miss is small enough to be luck.

    The idea

    Genetics predicts ratios, such as 3:1. Real counts never match exactly, so we need a rule for when the miss is too big. The chi-square statistic adds up how far each observed count is from the expected count, scaled by the expected count: χ² = Σ (o − e)² / e.

    Compare χ² with a critical value that depends on the degrees of freedom (df = number of categories − 1) and a significance level, usually p = 0.05. If χ² is less than the critical value, you fail to reject the null hypothesis: the data are consistent with the expected ratio and chance can explain the difference. If χ² is greater, you reject it: the difference is probably not just chance.

    Worked example

    A cross is expected to give 9 : 3 : 3 : 1. Among 160 offspring you count 94, 28, 26, 12. Test the fit.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Expected counts. 160 × 9/16 = 90, 160 × 3/16 = 30, 30, and 160/16 = 10.
    2. Each term (o − e)²/e. 16/90 = 0.18; 4/30 = 0.13; 16/30 = 0.53; 4/10 = 0.40.
    3. Add. χ² ≈ 1.24 with df = 4 − 1 = 3.
    4. Decide. The critical value for df 3 at p = 0.05 is 7.81. Since 1.24 < 7.81 you fail to reject the null: the data fit 9:3:3:1.

    Common mistakes

    • Using percentages or proportions instead of counts in the formula.
    • Computing expected from the observed totals incorrectly (expected must come from the ratio and the same total).
    • Using df = number of categories instead of categories − 1.
    • Saying "accept the null". You can only fail to reject it.
    • Thinking a small χ² proves the hypothesis. It means chance is a sufficient explanation.

    Writing the conclusion

    State the null hypothesis ("the observed ratio fits 3:1, deviations are due to chance"), give χ² and df, compare with the critical value, and conclude. "χ² = 3.1 < 3.84, so I fail to reject the null; the data are consistent with 3:1."

    Experimental design

    Every experiment is a fair test of one idea. If two things change at once, you cannot say which one mattered.

    The idea

    The independent variable is the one thing you change on purpose. The dependent variable is what you measure in response. A good test also has a control group that does not get the treatment (so you have something to compare with) and constants: everything else held the same, such as temperature, volume, time and source of organisms.

    Two more ideas make conclusions trustworthy. Replicates (several samples per group) let you see natural variation and compute error bars. A hypothesis is a testable prediction, often written "If ... then ... because ...". A conclusion must stay inside the data: say what changed, by how much, and what that supports, without claiming more than the design can show.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    4. Constants. Plant species and size, light intensity, distance from the lamp, temperature, water volume and bicarbonate concentration, and time. Replicates. Several plants per color.

    Common mistakes

    • Changing more than one variable at a time.
    • Calling the thing you measure the independent variable.
    • Having no control group, so there is nothing to compare with.
    • Using one trial and calling the result reliable.
    • Writing a conclusion that claims more than the data show (for example proving a mechanism the experiment did not test).

    Writing FRQ answers about design

    Use the words the question uses. "Identify" means name it. "Describe" means state what you would do or see. A strong design answer names the independent variable, the dependent variable you would measure and how, a control, at least two constants, and replicates. A prediction needs a reason: "If more fertilizer is added, growth will increase up to a point, because nitrogen limits protein synthesis."

    Rates, Q10 & dilutions

    Biology is full of "how fast": how fast a reaction runs, how fast a population grows, how diluted a solution is. A few formulas cover most of it.

    The idea

    A rate is a change per unit time: rate = Δquantity / Δtime. On a graph of quantity against time, the rate is the slope. To compare conditions, measure the same change over the same time.

    The temperature coefficient Q10 says how much a rate changes for a 10 °C rise: Q10 = (R2/R1)10/(T2 − T1). Many biological rates have Q10 near 2. This only holds below the temperature that denatures the enzymes. A serial dilution makes a solution weaker in equal steps: each step multiplies the dilution by the same factor.

    Water & hydrogen bonds

    Water is a small molecule that behaves like a much bigger one. Almost every property life relies on comes from one weak attraction between water molecules.

    The idea

    A water molecule is polar: oxygen pulls electrons harder than hydrogen does, so oxygen is slightly negative and the hydrogens slightly positive. The positive hydrogen of one molecule is attracted to the negative oxygen of another. This weak attraction is a hydrogen bond. One bond is weak and breaks easily, but liquid water has enormous numbers of them forming and breaking constantly.

    That network explains the properties on the exam. Cohesion (water sticks to water) gives surface tension and lets water be pulled up plant xylem; adhesion (water sticks to other polar surfaces) helps it climb narrow tubes. A high specific heat means water absorbs lots of energy for a small temperature change, so it buffers temperature. Ice is less dense than liquid water because bonds lock molecules into an open lattice, so ice floats. Polar and charged substances dissolve in water; nonpolar ones do not.

    • Mixing up cohesion (water to water) and adhesion (water to another surface).
    • Thinking ice floats because it is "lighter" in some other way. It is less dense because the lattice is more open.
    • Saying nonpolar molecules dissolve in water.
    • Treating hydrogen bonds as strong. Each is weak; the effect comes from the number.

    Macromolecules & monomers

    Cells build enormous molecules out of a few small repeating units, and they take them apart with the same chemistry in reverse.

    The idea

    There are four classes: carbohydrates (monosaccharides join into polysaccharides: starch, glycogen, cellulose), proteins (amino acids into polypeptides), nucleic acids (nucleotides into DNA and RNA) and lipids (fats are made of glycerol and fatty acids, but lipids are not true polymers). Each has a typical function: energy and structure, catalysis and structure, information, and long-term energy and membranes.

    Monomers are joined by dehydration synthesis: each bond forms as a water molecule is removed. Polymers are broken down by hydrolysis, which uses a water molecule to split each bond. The bonds are glycosidic (sugars), peptide (amino acids), phosphodiester (nucleotides) and ester (fats).

    • Mixing the names of the bonds.
    • Forgetting that cellulose and starch are both glucose polymers but differ in how the glucose units are linked, so they behave differently.

    Structure fits function

    Starch (plants) and glycogen (animals) store glucose; cellulose gives plant cell walls strength. Saturated fats have no double bonds and are solid at room temperature; unsaturated fats have double bonds that kink the chain and are liquid. Phospholipids have a hydrophilic head and hydrophobic tails, which is why they form bilayers.

    AP Unit 1

    Protein structure

    A protein is a chain of amino acids that folds into one particular shape, and the shape is what lets it work.

    The idea

    Twenty amino acids share the same backbone but differ in their R group: nonpolar (hydrophobic), polar, or charged. The order of amino acids is the primary structure. Hydrogen bonds along the backbone create secondary structure (α-helices and β-pleated sheets). Interactions among R groups (hydrophobic clustering, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges) fold the chain into a tertiary shape. Some proteins are made of several polypeptides: quaternary structure.

    Because folding depends on the R groups, changing even one amino acid can change the shape and function (sickle cell hemoglobin has one substitution). Heat, pH and salt can break the weak interactions that hold the shape: that is denaturation, and it usually destroys function.

      Interactions between R groups: hydrophobic clustering inside, hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, and covalent disulfide bridges between cysteines.

    2. At the quaternary stage, what is new?

      What you should see

      More than one polypeptide chain assembles into one functional protein, as in hemoglobin (four chains).

    Worked example

    Sickle cell hemoglobin has valine instead of glutamic acid at position 6 of the β chain. Explain how one change can change the protein.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Primary change. Glutamic acid has a charged R group; valine is nonpolar.
    2. Effect on interactions. The new nonpolar R group sticks to hydrophobic spots on other hemoglobin molecules.
    3. Effect on structure. Molecules clump into long fibers at low oxygen, changing the red blood cell shape.
    4. Conclusion. A single change in primary structure altered interactions, shape and function.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying peptide bonds hold the tertiary structure. They hold the primary structure; weak interactions and disulfides fold the chain.
    • Thinking denaturation breaks peptide bonds. It disrupts folding but usually leaves the sequence intact.
    • Forgetting that the R group determines where an amino acid sits in the folded protein.
    • Mixing secondary (backbone H-bonds) with tertiary (R-group interactions).
    • Assuming every protein has quaternary structure.

    Reading a data question

    If a mutation swaps a polar amino acid for a nonpolar one, predict where it will be (more likely inside, or on a surface where it disrupts solubility) and whether folding or binding is affected. Use "R group", "interaction", "shape" and "function" in your explanation.

    Check yourself: Which level of protein structure is the linear sequence of amino acids?

    AP Unit 1

    DNA & RNA structure

    DNA stores biological information in a simple pattern: two strands, four bases, and fixed pairing rules.

    The idea

    A nucleotide has a sugar (deoxyribose in DNA, ribose in RNA), a phosphate and a nitrogenous base. In DNA the bases are adenine (A), thymine (T), guanine (G) and cytosine (C). Nucleotides join by phosphodiester bonds between the sugar of one and the phosphate of the next, forming a sugar-phosphate backbone with a direction: one end is 5′ and the other is 3′.

    Two strands run antiparallel and are held by hydrogen bonds between bases: A pairs with T (two bonds) and G pairs with C (three bonds). So in double-stranded DNA, %A = %T and %G = %C (Chargaff's rules). RNA is usually single-stranded, uses ribose, and has uracil (U) in place of thymine.

      30% each: A = T and the rest (100 − 40 = 60%) splits evenly between A and T.

    Worked example

    A double-stranded DNA sample is 22% cytosine. What percentage is adenine? How many hydrogen bonds hold a 100 bp segment of it together?

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Chargaff. C = G = 22%, so G + C = 44%.
    2. A and T. The remaining 56% is split equally: A = T = 28%.
    3. Hydrogen bonds in 100 bp. 44 G–C pairs × 3 = 132 and 56 A–T pairs × 2 = 112, total 244.

    Common mistakes

    • Writing that A pairs with U in DNA.
    • Believing the strands run in the same direction.
    • Forgetting G–C has three bonds and A–T two.
    • Saying %A + %G = 50% is the same as %A = %G.
    • Mixing up deoxyribose and ribose.

    Applying Chargaff

    If you are given the percent of one base in double-stranded DNA you can find all four. In single-stranded DNA or RNA the rules do not apply. Compare melting temperatures by comparing G + C percentage: higher GC means more bonds and a higher melting temperature.

    AP Unit 2

    Diffusion & osmosis

    Nothing pushes a drop of dye through water. It spreads because molecules are always moving, and random motion has a direction once there are more molecules on one side.

    The idea

    Every molecule in a liquid is jostled constantly by thermal motion. Put more dye particles on the left than on the right and, just by chance, more of them wander left-to-right than right-to-left. The net movement is from high concentration to low concentration, and it stops (with the particles still moving) when the concentrations are equal. That is diffusion, and it is passive: it takes no energy from the cell.

    Osmosis is diffusion of water across a membrane that lets water through but holds back the solute. Water ends up moving toward the side with more solute, because that side has less free water. Compare two solutions by their solute concentration: the one with more solute is hypertonic, the one with less is hypotonic, equal is isotonic. An animal cell in a hypotonic solution swells (and can burst); in a hypertonic solution it shrinks. A plant cell has a wall: it becomes firm (turgid) in hypotonic solution and, in hypertonic solution, its membrane pulls away from the wall (plasmolysis).

      What you should see

      Toward the left, the side with more solute. The left level rises and the right falls until the extra height pushes back as hard as osmosis pulls.

    2. Switch to lets solute through. What equalizes now?

      What you should see

      The solute, not the water. Particles cross until the concentrations match (25 and 25 here), and the water levels stay equal because nothing osmotic is left to pull water.

    3. Put an animal cell in 0.05 M solute, then in 0.50 M. What happens in each?

      What you should see

      In 0.05 M (hypotonic) water rushes in and the cell lyses. In 0.50 M (hypertonic) water leaves and the cell shrivels. Only at 0.30 M (isotonic) does it keep its size.

    Worked example

    A potato core gains 8% of its mass in distilled water and loses 12% in 0.6 M sucrose. Estimate the solute concentration inside the potato cells, and say what the data show.

    Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

    1. Plot or tabulate. At 0 M the mass change is +8%; at 0.6 M it is −12%.
    2. Find where the change is zero. That concentration is isotonic to the cells: no net water movement.
    3. Interpolate linearly. The change falls by 20 percentage points over 0.6 M, so it falls 8 points over 0.6 × (8/20) = 0.24 M.
    4. Answer. The cells are isotonic to about 0.24 M sucrose. Below that the solution is hypotonic and the core gains water; above it, hypertonic, and the core loses water.

    Common mistakes

    • Saying water moves toward the side with more water. It moves toward the side with more solute (less free water).
    • Mixing up hypotonic and hypertonic: the prefix describes the solution relative to the cell (hyper = more solute).
    • Thinking diffusion stops when concentrations equalize. Particles keep moving; there is just no net movement.
    • Saying osmosis needs energy. It is passive.
    • Forgetting the cell wall: a plant cell cannot burst in hypotonic solution because the wall resists, which is why it becomes turgid.

    Calculations the exam likes

    Percent change in mass = (final − initial)/initial × 100, from potato or dialysis-bag labs. Plot percent change against solute concentration and read the x-intercept: that is the concentration isotonic to the tissue. Diffusion rate scales with surface area and the concentration difference and falls with distance: doubling the area doubles the rate, doubling the thickness halves it.
