Natural selection

Populations change because individuals that happen to fit their environment better leave more offspring. Nothing in the process looks ahead.

The idea

Natural selection needs four things: variation in a trait, the trait is heritable, more offspring are produced than can survive, and individuals with some trait values survive and reproduce more (higher fitness). Over generations the alleles associated with those traits become more common. Individuals do not evolve; populations do. Selection acts on phenotypes, but what changes is allele frequency.

The effect on a trait depends on which phenotypes are favored. Directional selection favors one extreme and shifts the mean (antibiotic resistance, finch beak depth in drought). Stabilizing selection favors the middle and narrows the spread (human birth weight). Disruptive selection favors both extremes and can split a population in two (seedcracker finch beaks). Other forces: sexual selection (traits that help win mates) and artificial selection (humans choose who breeds).

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose directional selection and press +10 generations. What happens to the mean?

    What you should see

    The mean shifts toward the favored extreme and the distribution keeps its shape.

  2. Choose disruptive selection and run 10 generations.

    What you should see

    Two peaks appear at the extremes and the middle becomes rare. The mean may stay about the same while the spread increases.

  3. In the resistance lab, make the antibiotic stronger (raise the selection).

    What you should see

    Resistance spreads in fewer generations: stronger selection means a bigger fitness difference.

Worked example

Before an antibiotic is used, 1 in 10,000 bacteria carry a resistance allele. After exposure, most sensitive bacteria die and survivors multiply. Explain how the population becomes resistant without any bacterium "deciding" to.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Variation. Resistance already exists in a few cells (from mutation).
  2. Selection. The antibiotic kills sensitive cells but not resistant ones, so resistant cells have higher fitness.
  3. Heredity. Survivors pass the allele to their offspring.
  4. Result. The frequency of the resistant allele rises each generation. The change is in the population, not in individual cells.

Common mistakes

  • Saying individuals evolve or adapt during their lifetime.
  • Saying organisms change "because they need to".
  • Treating fitness as strength instead of reproductive success.
  • Forgetting that selection needs heritable variation.
  • Saying mutations are caused by the environment to help the organism (they arise randomly; the environment selects).

Writing the evolution explanation

Use the four steps: variation, heritability, differential survival and reproduction, change in allele frequency over generations. Name the selective pressure and the favored phenotype, and say which mode of selection applies.
