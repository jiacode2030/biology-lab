Enzymes & activation energy

Reactions in cells would be far too slow at body temperature without help. Enzymes make them fast enough to sustain life, and do it one reaction at a time.

The idea

An enzyme is a protein catalyst. It binds its substrate at the active site, whose shape and chemistry fit that substrate (the active site adjusts slightly on binding, induced fit). Enzymes speed reactions by lowering the activation energy (the energy hurdle to get started). They do not change the free energy of the reactants or products, do not make an unfavorable reaction favorable, and are not used up.

Reaction rate rises with substrate concentration until all active sites are busy (saturation), then levels off at Vmax. A competitive inhibitor resembles the substrate and blocks the active site; more substrate can outcompete it, so Vmax can still be reached. A noncompetitive (allosteric) inhibitor binds elsewhere and changes the shape of the enzyme, lowering the Vmax that can be reached.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose competitive inhibition, then raise substrate to 100. What happens to the rate?

    What you should see

    It climbs back almost to the uninhibited maximum: lots of substrate outcompetes the inhibitor.

  2. Choose noncompetitive inhibition and raise substrate to 100. Does it recover?

    What you should see

    No. The ceiling is lower because some enzyme molecules are altered whatever the substrate level.

  3. Add the enzyme to the energy profile. What changes and what does not?

    What you should see

    The peak (activation energy) falls. The start and end levels, and so ΔG, stay the same.

Worked example

A reaction has an activation energy of 80 kJ/mol and ΔG = −20 kJ/mol. An enzyme lowers the activation energy to 45 kJ/mol. State the new activation energy and ΔG, and the effect on rate.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Activation energy. 45 kJ/mol (lowered by 35).
  2. ΔG. Still −20 kJ/mol. An enzyme cannot change the energy of reactants or products.
  3. Rate. Faster, because a larger fraction of molecules has enough energy to react. Equilibrium is reached sooner but is not moved.

Common mistakes

  • Saying enzymes add energy to the reaction.
  • Saying enzymes make a nonspontaneous reaction spontaneous.
  • Believing enzymes are used up.
  • Mixing competitive and noncompetitive inhibitors.
  • Forgetting that rate levels off because enzyme is limiting, not substrate.

Reading rate graphs

On a rate vs. substrate graph, a flat region means the enzyme is saturated: adding substrate does nothing, adding enzyme would. A rising region means substrate is limiting. If the inhibited curve reaches the same maximum, the inhibitor is competitive; if the maximum is lower, it is noncompetitive.
