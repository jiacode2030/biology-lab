Mendelian inheritance

Offspring get one allele for each gene from each parent. With a Punnett square you can predict the odds before anything is born.

The idea

Mendel's law of segregation: the two alleles for a gene separate into different gametes, so each gamete carries one. A dominant allele shows when present; a recessive allele shows only in two copies. Genotype is the allele combination (AA, Aa, aa); phenotype is the observable trait. A cross of two heterozygotes (Aa × Aa) gives genotypes 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa and phenotypes 3 dominant : 1 recessive.

The law of independent assortment: genes on different chromosomes are inherited independently, so AaBb × AaBb gives a 9 : 3 : 3 : 1 phenotype ratio. A test cross (unknown × homozygous recessive) reveals an unknown genotype. Use the product rule (and) and the sum rule (or) for probabilities. Real counts only approximate the ratios, which is where the chi-square test comes in.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Set both parents to Aa. What phenotype ratio results?

    What you should see

    3 dominant : 1 recessive (genotypes 1 AA : 2 Aa : 1 aa).

  2. Make parent 1 Aa and parent 2 aa (a test cross). What do you see?

    What you should see

    1 : 1 for both genotype and phenotype. A homozygous dominant parent would instead give all dominant offspring.

  3. Highlight "recessive for both". What fraction of the 16?

    What you should see

    1/16. The four classes are 9 : 3 : 3 : 1.

Worked example

In pea plants purple (P) is dominant to white (p). Two heterozygous purple plants are crossed. What is the probability that the first two offspring are both white?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. One offspring. Pp × Pp gives 1/4 pp (white).
  2. Product rule. P(both white) = 1/4 × 1/4.
  3. Answer. 1/16 = 0.0625 (6.25%). Offspring are independent events, so earlier results do not change later odds.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the "ratio" must appear exactly in real data.
  • Thinking a family with three dominant children makes the next child likely recessive.
  • Mixing up genotype and phenotype ratios.
  • Forgetting each parent contributes one allele.
  • Using the sum rule when "and" requires the product rule.

Reading a cross

First write the parent genotypes (use the phenotype of offspring to infer them). Then list gametes, fill the square, and count. For two genes, treat each separately and multiply: P(AaBb) = P(Aa) × P(Bb). Give answers as ratios or probabilities as asked.

Check yourself: Two parents are both Aa. What fraction of their offspring is expected to show the recessive phenotype?
