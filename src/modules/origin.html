Origins of cell compartments

Mitochondria and chloroplasts look like small bacteria living inside our cells, and the evidence says that is close to what they are.

The idea

The endosymbiotic theory proposes that mitochondria arose when an ancestral cell engulfed an aerobic bacterium that survived and became a permanent partner, and that chloroplasts arose later when a cell engulfed a photosynthetic cyanobacterium. The nucleus and endomembrane system probably arose by infolding of the plasma membrane.

Evidence: both organelles have a double membrane, their own circular DNA, their own ribosomes similar to bacterial ribosomes, and they reproduce by binary fission independently of the cell. Prokaryotes lack membrane-bound organelles and have a nucleoid instead of a nucleus; eukaryotes have both.

Worked example

A researcher finds that an organelle has circular DNA, ribosomes that are sensitive to an antibiotic that stops bacterial protein synthesis, and divides by binary fission. What can be inferred?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Compare with bacteria. Those are all bacterial features.
  2. Inference. The organelle likely descended from a free-living bacterium that was engulfed: the endosymbiotic theory.
  3. Strength. This is evidence consistent with the theory, not proof; combine it with DNA sequence similarity for stronger support.

Common mistakes

  • Saying mitochondria and chloroplasts were made by the nucleus.
  • Thinking every organelle came from endosymbiosis. The nucleus and ER are thought to come from infolding.
  • Saying prokaryotes have no DNA.
  • Writing that the theory has been proven rather than strongly supported.
  • Mixing up which organelle came first (mitochondria first in the usual account).

Evidence language

Use "consistent with" for evidence. Name at least two features and link each to bacteria: circular DNA, bacterial-type ribosomes, binary fission, double membrane (from engulfing).
