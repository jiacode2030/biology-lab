Feedback & homeostasis

Living things keep conditions steady by measuring them and correcting any drift. Occasionally they do the opposite on purpose.

The idea

Negative feedback reverses a change and brings a variable back to its set point: body temperature, blood glucose, blood pH and water balance. A sensor detects a change, a control center compares it with the set point, and an effector acts to oppose it. For blood glucose, high glucose makes the pancreas release insulin (cells take up glucose, liver stores glycogen); low glucose makes it release glucagon (liver releases glucose).

Positive feedback amplifies the change and pushes the system away from where it started until something ends it. Examples: oxytocin and uterine contractions during childbirth, ripening fruit releasing ethylene that speeds ripening, and blood clotting. Positive feedback is not a failure of homeostasis; it drives a process to completion. Without any feedback a variable drifts toward the surroundings.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose negative feedback with the surroundings at 15 °C. What happens to the value?

    What you should see

    It settles near the set point despite the cold: the effector (heat production) opposes the drift.

  2. Choose positive feedback. What happens?

    What you should see

    The value runs away from the set point (up or down), because each change makes the next change bigger.

Worked example

Blood glucose rises after a meal. Describe the feedback loop that returns it to normal.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Sensor. Pancreatic beta cells detect high blood glucose.
  2. Response. They release insulin into the blood.
  3. Effectors. Body cells take up glucose and the liver converts it to glycogen.
  4. Result. Blood glucose falls toward the set point and insulin release decreases: negative feedback.

Common mistakes

  • Calling negative feedback "bad". It means the response opposes the change.
  • Thinking positive feedback means a good result.
  • Saying positive feedback maintains homeostasis. It amplifies a change.
  • Forgetting that insulin and glucagon act oppositely.
  • Not identifying the effector.

Naming the loop on the exam

Say what changed, what detected it, what responded, and whether the response reduced the change (negative) or increased it (positive). Label examples: temperature, glucose, blood pressure (negative); childbirth, fruit ripening, clotting (positive).
