Temperature, pH & enzyme activity

Enzymes work best in a narrow range. Move out of it and the protein changes shape and the reaction slows or stops.

The idea

Each enzyme has an optimum temperature and optimum pH fitted to the place it works: pepsin works at about pH 2 in the stomach, trypsin at about pH 8 in the small intestine, and the polymerase from hot-spring bacteria works near 72 °C. Rates rise with temperature because molecules collide more often and with more energy; past the optimum the enzyme denatures and the rate falls steeply.

pH changes the charges on amino acid R groups, which changes ionic and hydrogen bonds and so the active site. Extreme pH can denature the enzyme. These effects show up on exam graphs as bell-shaped curves; the peak marks the optimum. A graph with temperature on the x-axis that rises, peaks and then drops quickly is the typical pattern.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose pepsin and set pH to 2 at 37 °C. Then raise the pH to 8.

    What you should see

    Activity is near the maximum at pH 2 and almost zero at pH 8: pepsin is adapted to stomach acid.

  2. Choose Taq and run it at 37 °C and at 72 °C.

    What you should see

    At 37 °C it is slow (cold for this enzyme); at 72 °C it is at its best, and a human enzyme at 72 °C would be denatured.

Worked example

An enzyme gives these relative rates: 20 °C: 25, 30 °C: 50, 40 °C: 100, 50 °C: 30, 60 °C: 0. Estimate the optimum and explain the drop above it.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Optimum. The highest rate is at 40 °C (100).
  2. Rise. From 20 to 40 °C the rate doubles each 10 °C, as expected from more frequent and energetic collisions (Q10 ≈ 2).
  3. Drop. Above 40 °C the enzyme begins to denature: heat breaks the weak interactions that fold the active site, so fewer enzymes can bind substrate. By 60 °C most are denatured.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the enzyme "dies". Enzymes are not alive; they denature.
  • Thinking the optimum is the same for all enzymes.
  • Saying peptide bonds break on mild heating.
  • Forgetting pH also changes enzyme shape.
  • Describing a high rate at a high temperature as an adaptation without noting the organism.

Interpreting graphs

Name the optimum from the peak, describe the rise (collisions, kinetic energy) and the fall (denaturation: shape lost, active site no longer fits), and link the optimum to the environment the enzyme works in.
