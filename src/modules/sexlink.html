Sex linkage & pedigrees

Genes on the X chromosome behave differently in males and females, because males have only one X.

The idea

Females are XX and males are XY. A gene on the X chromosome with no matching gene on the Y is X-linked. Males have one allele, so a single recessive allele (for red-green color blindness or hemophilia) shows. Females need two. A carrier female (XHXh) is unaffected. Sons inherit their X only from their mother and their Y from the father, so X-linked traits are never passed from father to son, and fathers pass their X to all daughters.

A pedigree is a family tree showing a trait (squares are males, circles are females, filled symbols are affected). Clues: autosomal recessive: two unaffected parents can have an affected child; both sexes equally affected. Autosomal dominant: every affected person has an affected parent; no skipped generations. X-linked recessive: many more affected males; affected males have unaffected father; passes through carrier daughters.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Choose a carrier mother and a normal father. What are the odds for sons?

    What you should see

    1/2 of sons are affected (they got Xh), 1/2 are normal. Half of daughters are carriers, none affected.

  2. Choose an affected father (XʰY) and a normal mother (XᴴXᴴ).

    What you should see

    No sons are affected (they get his Y); all daughters are carriers (they get his Xʰ).

Worked example

A woman who is a carrier for hemophilia (X-linked recessive) has children with an unaffected man. What is the probability that a child (sex unknown) has hemophilia?

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. Cross. XHXh × XHY gives XHXH, XHXh, XHY, XhY.
  2. Affected. Only XhY: 1 of 4.
  3. Answer. 1/4 (25%) for a child of unknown sex; for a son it is 1/2.

Common mistakes

  • Saying a father can pass an X-linked allele to his son.
  • Writing a recessive X-linked trait as if females were affected as often as males.
  • Mixing up carriers (unaffected) with affected females.
  • Reading a pedigree and forgetting the trait could be autosomal.
  • Forgetting the question might ask about the probability for a son vs for any child.

Reading pedigrees

Look first for two unaffected parents with an affected child: that points to recessive. Then ask whether more males are affected and whether affected males have unaffected fathers: that points to X-linked. Confirm by assigning genotypes and checking every mating.
