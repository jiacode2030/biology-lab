Membrane structure & permeability

A cell membrane is a thin oily film with proteins floating in it. It is selective because of what it is made of, not because it has holes.

The idea

The membrane is a phospholipid bilayer: each phospholipid has a hydrophilic head and two hydrophobic tails, so in water the tails hide in the middle. Proteins are embedded (integral) or attached (peripheral); carbohydrates on the outer surface help cells recognize each other. The pieces move around within the layer, which is why it is called a fluid mosaic. Cholesterol (in animal cells) buffers fluidity: it keeps the membrane from packing too tightly when cold and from becoming too loose when warm.

Because the middle is nonpolar, small nonpolar molecules (O₂, CO₂) cross easily. Small polar molecules like water cross slowly (aquaporins speed it up). Ions and large polar molecules (glucose) need channel or carrier proteins. Very large particles move by endocytosis and exocytosis.

Try this

Predict what will happen first. Then set it up and check.

  1. Remove cholesterol and cool to 5 °C. What happens to fluidity?

    What you should see

    Fluidity is very low: phospholipids pack tightly. With cholesterol present the membrane stays more fluid at this temperature.

  2. Choose Na⁺. Why can't it cross directly?

    What you should see

    Ions are charged and cannot pass through the hydrophobic interior. They need channel or carrier proteins.

Worked example

A protein is found to span the membrane, with a hydrophobic section in the middle and hydrophilic ends. Explain why.

Try it yourself first, then reveal one step at a time.

  1. The interior is nonpolar. The fatty acid tails form a hydrophobic core.
  2. The middle section. Its nonpolar amino acids interact with the tails, anchoring the protein in the bilayer.
  3. The ends. Polar and charged amino acids face the watery cytoplasm and extracellular fluid.

Common mistakes

  • Saying the membrane is static. It is a moving fluid mosaic.
  • Saying water crosses only through channels. Some crosses directly, slowly.
  • Thinking cholesterol makes the membrane rigid at all temperatures.
  • Putting the hydrophilic heads in the middle.
  • Forgetting the carbohydrate groups on the outside.

Predicting permeability

Ask two questions: is it small and nonpolar (crosses directly), and is it charged or large (needs a protein or vesicle)? Then decide whether energy is needed: moving down a gradient is passive, up a gradient needs ATP.
